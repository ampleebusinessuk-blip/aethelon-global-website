The adaptive era: making long-term moves in short-term cycles

By Strategy practice

October 8, 2026 · 5 min read
LinkedIn X (Twitter) Email

Quarterly plans and multi-year bets do not have to compete. Portfolio thinking and clear guardrails let leaders adapt quickly without losing sight of the long game.

Leaders today face an awkward tension. Markets, technology and customer expectations shift so quickly that annual plans feel out of date by the time they are approved. At the same time, the moves that create lasting advantage, such as building a new platform, entering a new market or reshaping a workforce, take years to pay off.

Many organizations respond by swinging between two extremes. Some lock in long-range plans and struggle to change course. Others become so focused on the next quarter that they never commit to anything big. Neither works well. The organizations that thrive in what we call the adaptive era learn to make long-term moves in short-term cycles.

Quarterly planning and multi-year bets are not opposites

Short planning cycles have real benefits. They force teams to deliver something tangible, surface problems early and let leaders shift resources as conditions change. But on their own, quarterly cycles tend to favor work that shows results quickly. Over time, that bias quietly starves the longer-term investments that matter most.

The answer is not to choose between short and long. It is to connect them. A multi-year bet should be broken into a sequence of quarterly steps, each of which reduces uncertainty, builds capability or delivers value on its own. Every quarter becomes a checkpoint for the bet, not a threat to it.

A long-term strategy is not a fixed plan. It is a clear direction, revisited often, with the discipline to keep going when the early results are quiet.

Thinking in portfolios, not projects

One of the most useful shifts a leadership team can make is to manage its initiatives as a portfolio. Rather than judging each project in isolation, you look at the mix and ask whether it reflects the future you are trying to build.

A simple way to structure a portfolio is in three horizons:

  • Run and improve: work that strengthens the core business today, such as efficiency programs and incremental product improvements
  • Grow and extend: work that expands into adjacent customers, channels or offerings over the next one to three years
  • Explore and bet: smaller, higher-risk experiments that could shape the business further out

There is no perfect ratio, and it will vary by industry and appetite for risk. What matters is that the mix is a deliberate choice, reviewed regularly, rather than the accidental result of whichever teams argued most persuasively in the last budget round.

Portfolio thinking also makes it easier to stop things. When every initiative is weighed against the others, leaders can redirect funding from work that has stalled to work that is gaining traction, without treating each decision as a failure.

Guardrails that make speed safe

Moving quickly is only valuable if teams know where the edges are. Guardrails give teams the freedom to make decisions locally while protecting the organization from unwelcome surprises. Good guardrails are few in number, easy to understand and agreed in advance.

Examples we often see work well include:

  1. Strategic boundaries. A short list of things the organization will and will not do, such as markets it will not enter or customer promises it will not break.
  2. Funding thresholds. Clear limits on how much a team can spend before it needs a wider conversation, paired with fast routes to more funding when evidence supports it.
  3. Risk and compliance standards. Non-negotiable requirements that are built into how work is done, not checked only at the end.
  4. Decision rights. Clarity on who can make which calls, so that teams are not waiting on approvals that add no value.

Getting these right is often a question of operating model design. When structures, funding and governance support adaptive planning, strategy stops being an annual event and becomes an ongoing practice.

Putting it into practice

If you want to start building this muscle, a few practical steps can help:

  • Write down your three to five long-term bets in plain language, with the outcome each is meant to deliver
  • For each bet, define what you expect to learn or achieve in the next quarter
  • Hold a quarterly portfolio review where you look at the whole mix, not just individual projects
  • Agree on the signals that would cause you to double down, change course or stop
  • Communicate the direction often, so teams understand how their short-term work connects to the long game

A common pitfall is treating the quarterly review as a status update. The real value comes from honest conversations about what you have learned and whether your bets still make sense. That takes trust, and it takes practice, which is why thoughtful change enablement matters as much as the planning process itself.
